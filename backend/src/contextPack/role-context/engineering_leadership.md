# Role context — Engineering Management & Leadership

Hand-maintained and public-safe, like `company-profile.md`, and deliberately NOT
run through the sanitizer (see the note in `product.md`). No headcount numbers,
no budgets, no compensation, no individual performance detail. Candidates are
never quizzed on it; it exists so questions match the actual job.

Covers Engineering Manager, Senior EM, Director and Head of Engineering. It does
NOT cover Staff, Principal or Tech Lead — those are individual-contributor
craft roles and should be interviewed as such.

## What the job actually is here

Engineering is organised as small autonomous pods, each owning a product or a
slice of one, across a house of separate consumer apps. Priorities shift, pods
get re-pointed, and people move between products. A manager here owns delivery
and quality for their area while the roadmap underneath them is still moving.

The distinctive demand is **breadth without ownership of a single stable
domain**: leaders are expected to be effective across products they did not
build and do not know well yet.

## What makes it harder than it sounds

- **Multi-product context switching is the default**, not an exception. A leader
  who needs deep domain familiarity before they can be useful will struggle.
- **Cost is a real constraint on technical decisions.** Infrastructure is sized
  deliberately small; "scale the box up" is available but rarely the first
  answer, and a leader is expected to hold that line without demoralising the
  team.
- **Reliability has human consequences.** An outage is not an inconvenient
  dashboard — it interrupts someone's job application or a farmer's advisory.
- **Hiring happens in a competitive market** against product companies with
  deeper pockets, so growing people internally matters as much as recruiting.
- **The roadmap changes.** Sequencing judgement and the ability to re-plan
  without churn matter more than long-range plans.
- **Directors lead through EMs and Staff engineers**, so their leverage is
  operating cadence, org design and the quality of their leaders — not their own
  technical output.

## The kind of problem worth reasoning about

Situational and grounded in the day-to-day. For managers:

- A delivery that has been slipping quarter over quarter, and how they diagnosed
  whether it was scope, capability, or planning.
- An engineer who is technically strong and difficult to work with.
- Deciding when to open a performance conversation, and what they tried first.
- On-call health degrading, and what they changed.
- Being asked to take on a product area they know nothing about.
- Pushing back on a roadmap commitment they believe is unachievable.

For directors, the same shape one level up: what broke as the org grew, how they
found out, how they decide between right-sizing infrastructure and re-architecting,
how they defend a headcount or cost trade-off, and how they judge whether an EM
reporting to them is actually effective.

## How they are expected to work

- Ownership over process: their area's outcomes are theirs, including the
  unglamorous parts.
- Honesty about limits, including about their own mistakes and hires that did
  not work.
- Hard feedback delivered kindly — the JD language is "honest but never unkind".
- Pragmatism: the answer accounts for the constraint that exists.
- Curiosity about the user, not just about delivery metrics.
